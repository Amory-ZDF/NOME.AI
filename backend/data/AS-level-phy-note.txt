CH1: Physical Quantities and Units
1. Physical Quantities
A physical quantity consists of a numerical value and a unit (e.g., length l = 4 m).
Estimation is a skill used to approximate values, compare quantities, or check if a calculated result is reasonable.
2. SI Units
Base SI quantities and units:
Mass – kilogram (kg)
Length – metre (m)
Time – second (s)
Current – ampere (A)
Temperature – kelvin (K)
Amount of substance – mole (mol) (A‑level only)
Derived units are obtained from equations. Example: F = m a → SI unit of force = kg × m s^-2 = kg m s^-2 (newton, N).
A homogeneous equation has the same units on both sides. All valid physics equations are homogeneous; this can be used to check formulas.
3. Prefixes



Examples: 6 pF = 6 × 10^-12 F; 10 μm = 10 × 10^-6 m.
4. Precision, Accuracy & Uncertainty
Precision: measurements are consistent (small scatter about a mean), but does not indicate accuracy.
Accuracy: a measurement close to the true value.
Absolute uncertainty: fixed quantity, e.g. 7 ± 0.6 V.
Fractional uncertainty: e.g. 7 ± 3/35 V.
Percentage uncertainty: e.g. 7 ± 8.6 % V.
To reduce fractional/percentage uncertainty, measure larger quantities.
Reading (one value) – uncertainty = ± half the smallest division (e.g., thermometer ±0.5 °C).
Measurement (difference of two readings) – uncertainty = at least ±1 smallest division (e.g., ruler ±1 mm, because both ends have ±0.5 mm added).
5. Combining Uncertainties
Adding / subtracting → add absolute uncertainties.
Example: temperature drop from 298 ± 0.5 K to 273 ± 0.5 K → difference = 25 ± 1 K.
Multiplying / dividing → add percentage uncertainties.
Example: force 91 ± 3 N, mass 7 ± 0.2 kg → acceleration requires adding percentage uncertainties of force and mass.
6. Scalars and Vectors
Scalars: magnitude only (e.g., distance, speed, mass, temperature).
Vectors: magnitude and direction (e.g., displacement, velocity, force/weight, acceleration).
Adding vectors
Perpendicular vectors – use Pythagoras and trigonometry.
Example: 5 N and 12 N perpendicular → resultant R = sqrt(12^2 + 5^2) = 13 N, direction theta = tan^-1(5/12) = 22.6° from horizontal.
Non‑perpendicular vectors – use scale drawing (ruler and protractor) or resolve into components.
Resolution: x = V cos(theta), y = V sin(theta). Remember: if moving through the angle theta to the component, use cos; if moving away from the angle, use sin.
Example: velocity 10 m/s at 30° to horizontal → vertical component = 10 sin30° = 5 m/s, horizontal = 10 cos30° ≈ 8.66 m/s.
Scale drawing example: ship 30 m at bearing 060° then 20 m east → displacement magnitude ≈ 49 m, bearing ≈ 072° (by drawing).

Topic 2: Kinematics
2.1 - Equations of Motion
Distance – scalar quantity, describes the amount of ground covered.
Displacement (s) – overall distance from starting position, includes direction (vector).
Speed – scalar, distance travelled per unit time.
Velocity (v) – rate of change of displacement → v = Δs / Δt
Acceleration (a) – rate of change of velocity → a = Δv / Δt
Uniform acceleration – acceleration is constant.
Graphs:
acceleration-time graph – area under graph = change in velocity.
velocity-time graph – gradient = acceleration; area under graph = displacement.
displacement-time graph – gradient = velocity.
Instantaneous velocity – velocity at a specific point in time. Found from displacement-time graph by drawing a tangent at that time and calculating its gradient.
Distance-time and speed-time graphs – distance and speed are scalars, so they are never negative.

Derivation of uniform acceleration equations (from velocity-time graph):
Area under v-t graph = displacement
s = area of rectangle + area of triangle
s = u t + (t/2)(v - u)
Simplify → s = (u + v)/2 × t
Gradient of v-t graph = acceleration
a = (v - u) / t
Rearrange → v = u + a t
Another way using area
s = u t + (t/2)(v - u)
Substitute at = v - u → s = u t + (t/2)(a t)
→ s = u t + (1/2) a t^2
Final equation (substitute t = (v - u)/a into s = (u + v)/2 × t)
s = (u + v)/2 × (v - u)/a
s = (v^2 - u^2) / (2a)
→ v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s
Summary of uniform acceleration formulas:
v = u + a t
s = (u + v)/2 × t
s = u t + (1/2) a t^2
v^2 = u^2 + 2 a s
Where:
s = displacement
u = initial velocity
v = final velocity
a = acceleration
t = time
Tip: when solving problems, write down known values and the one you need to find, then choose the appropriate formula.
Example (stone dropped from 50 m bridge):
Given: s = 50 m, u = 0 m/s, a = 9.81 m/s^2 (acceleration due to gravity, g). Find v and t.
Using v^2 = u^2 + 2as → v^2 = 0 + 2 × 9.81 × 50 = 981 → v = 31.3 m/s
Using s = u t + (1/2) a t^2 → 50 = 0 + 0.5 × 9.81 × t^2 → t^2 = 10.19 → t = 3.19 s

Experiment to measure g (using light gates):
Set up apparatus with two light gates connected to a data logger, close to an electromagnet.
Adjust lower light gate so height h = 0.75 m (measured with metre ruler).
Turn on electromagnet, attach ball bearing.
Switch off magnet, record time t for bearing to fall between gates.
Reduce h by 0.05 m each time (down to 0.25 m), repeat.
Repeat twice more to get mean t for each h.
Analysis:
Initial velocity u = 0, displacement s = h, acceleration = g.
From s = u t + (1/2) a t^2 → h = (1/2) g t^2 → 2h = g t^2
So a graph of 2h against t^2 (using mean t^2 values) will have gradient = g.

Projectile motion (parabolic path):
Object moves at constant horizontal velocity (v_x) while experiencing uniform vertical acceleration (due to gravity).
Vertical velocity (v_y) increases with time, while horizontal velocity remains constant.
The combination causes the direction to change increasingly over time, producing a parabolic trajectory.

Topic 3: Dynamics
3.1 - Momentum and Newton's Laws of Motion
Newton's 1st law – An object will remain at rest or travelling at a constant velocity, until it experiences a resultant force.
Newton's 2nd law – The acceleration of an object is proportional to the resultant force experienced: F = m a. Resultant force and acceleration are always in the same direction.
Newton's 3rd law – For each force experienced by an object, the object exerts an equal and opposite force.
Mass – property of a body, measure of its resistance to acceleration. Larger mass → smaller acceleration for a given resultant force.
Example: Object 1 (m = 1 kg) and Object 2 (m = 10 kg), both with resultant force 10 N.
Object 1: a = 10 / 1 = 10 m s^-2
Object 2: a = 10 / 10 = 1 m s^-2
Linear momentum = mass × velocity (p = m v).
From Newton's 2nd law: F = m a = Δ(m v) / Δ t → Force is the rate of change of momentum.
Weight – gravitational force on an object, varies with gravitational field strength. On Earth: Weight = mass × g (where g = acceleration due to gravity, ~9.81 m s^-2).

3.2 - Non-Uniform Motion (e.g. falling with air resistance)
For an object falling in a uniform gravitational field (initial velocity = 0):
Weight acts downwards, air resistance acts upwards. Weight > air resistance → resultant force downwards → object accelerates (Newton's 2nd law).
As velocity increases, air resistance increases → resultant force decreases → acceleration decreases.
Eventually, air resistance = weight → resultant force = 0 → object moves at constant terminal velocity.
Air resistance affects projectile motion:
Maximum height is reached earlier.
Both vertical and horizontal distances travelled are reduced.

3.3 - Linear Momentum and its Conservation
Principle of conservation of momentum – total momentum is conserved in any interaction where no external forces act (momentum before = momentum after).
Example (perfectly inelastic collision):
Car (500 kg) at 4 m s^-1 collides with stationary truck (1500 kg). They join and move with velocity V.
Momentum before = (500 × 4) + (1500 × 0) = 2000 kg m s^-1.
Momentum after = (500 + 1500) × V = 2000 V.
2000 = 2000 V → V = 1 m s^-1.
Types of collisions:
Elastic – both momentum and kinetic energy are conserved.
Inelastic – only momentum is conserved; some kinetic energy is converted to other forms (heat, sound, deformation, etc.).
For a perfectly elastic collision: relative speed of approach = relative speed of separation.
Example: two equal-mass steel balls, one at rest. Moving ball collides and transfers all momentum and kinetic energy to the stationary ball; the first ball comes to rest, the second moves off with the same speed.
Most collisions are inelastic – e.g., vehicle collisions: kinetic energy → elastic potential energy (deformation of crumple zones) + thermal energy + sound energy.

Topic 4: Forces, Density and Pressure
4.1 - Types of Force
Uniform gravitational field – exerts the same gravitational force on a mass everywhere in the field.
Force (weight) can be calculated using: F = m g
m = mass of the object
g = gravitational field strength (constant in a uniform field)
On Earth's surface, g ≈ 9.81 N kg^-1
Centre of gravity – the point at which an object's weight acts.
For a uniform object, the centre of gravity is exactly at its geometric centre.

4.2 - Equilibrium of Forces
Moment of a force about a point = Force × perpendicular distance from the line of action of the force to the point.
Couple – a pair of coplanar forces (within the same plane), equal in magnitude but opposite in direction. A couple produces rotation only (no translation).
Torque of a couple = Force × perpendicular distance between the lines of action of the forces.
Principle of moments – for an object in equilibrium, the sum of anticlockwise moments about any pivot equals the sum of clockwise moments.
Example (finding F from a diagram, taking moments about point A):
(2 × 50) + (3 × 35) = (3.5 × F)
205 = 3.5 F → F = 58.6 N
Note: moments taken about point A mean the distance from A to A is 0, so the moment caused by any force acting at A is zero and can be ignored.
A system is in equilibrium if:
No resultant force (net force = 0)
No resultant torque (net torque = 0)
How to show an object is in equilibrium:
Method 1: Resolve all forces into horizontal and vertical components – if both sums equal zero, the object is in equilibrium.
Method 2 (for 3 coplanar forces): Draw a scale diagram – if it forms a closed triangle, the object is in equilibrium.

4.3 - Density and Pressure
Density (ρ) = mass per unit volume → ρ = m / V
m = mass, V = volume. Density measures how compact a substance is.
Pressure (p) = force perpendicular to a surface per unit area → p = F / A
F = force acting perpendicular to surface, A = area.

Derivation of Δp = ρ g Δh:
Consider a fluid column of density ρ, height h, and cross-sectional area A.
Volume of fluid column = A × h
Mass of fluid column = ρ × A × h
Weight of fluid column = ρ × A × h × g
Pressure at the bottom = Weight / Area = (ρ × A × h × g) / A = ρ h g
Therefore, the pressure difference between two points in a fluid separated by height Δh is:Δp = ρ g Δh

Total pressure vs pressure due to fluid:
The equation Δp = ρ g Δh gives the pressure due to the fluid alone (not total pressure).
Total pressure = pressure due to fluid + atmospheric pressure.
Example (stone at bottom of lake):
Depth h = 15.0 m, water density ρ = 997 kg m^-3, atmospheric pressure = 1.01 × 10^5 Pa.
Pressure from water = ρ g h = 997 × 9.81 × 15.0 = 1.47 × 10^5 Pa.
Total pressure = 1.47 × 10^5 + 1.01 × 10^5 = 2.48 × 10^5 Pa.

Upthrust (buoyant force):
For a submerged object, pressure at the bottom is greater than pressure at the top (because depth h is larger at the bottom).
Since p = F / A and the areas are equal, the upward force on the bottom is greater than the downward force on the top → resultant upward force (upthrust).
Archimedes' Principle – the weight of the fluid displaced by a submerged body equals the upthrust force exerted on the body.
Buoyant force F = ρ g V
ρ = density of the fluid
g = gravitational acceleration
V = volume of fluid displaced (equal to the volume of the submerged part of the object)

Topic 5: Work, Energy and Power
5.1 - Energy Conservation
Energy – defined as the capacity to do work.
Work done (W) = force causing motion × distance travelled in the direction of the motion.
W = F s cosθ
s = distance travelled
θ = angle between the direction of the force and the direction of motion
Work done on a gas at constant pressure (usually by transferring thermal energy):
Work done = p ΔV
p = pressure
ΔV = change in volume
Efficiency – a measure of how efficiently a system transfers energy.
Efficiency = useful output power / input power
Percentage efficiency = (useful output power / input power) × 100%
Efficiency is never 100% – energy is always lost to friction (thermal, sound, etc.).
Principle of conservation of energy – energy cannot be created or destroyed, only transferred from one form to another. Total energy in a closed system stays constant.
Total energy in = Total energy out
Example: ball thrown upwards – kinetic energy → gravitational potential energy (at max height), then GPE → KE on the way down.
Important: due to air resistance, initial KE ≠ maximum GPE (some energy is transferred to the environment as heat).
Power (P) – the rate of energy transfer (energy transferred per unit time). Since work is a measure of energy transfer:
P = ΔW / Δt = (F × Δs) / Δt = F v (since v = Δs / Δt)
Energy transferred = P × Δt

Example questions:
Kettle boiling water:
Energy required = 163800 J, kettle power = 1200 W.
P = W / t → t = W / P = 163800 / 1200 = 136.5 s.
Ball gaining kinetic energy:
Ball mass 0.6 kg, accelerates from rest to 12 m s^-1 in 0.2 s.
Kinetic energy gained = (1/2) × 0.6 × 12^2 = 43.2 J.
Average power = 43.2 / 0.2 = 216 W.
Car at constant velocity:
Car travels at 15 m s^-1 against frictional force of 200 N.
Constant velocity → resultant force = 0 → engine force = 200 N (Newton's 1st law).
Power = F v = 200 × 15 = 3000 W.

5.2 - Gravitational Potential Energy and Kinetic Energy
Kinetic Energy (E_k) = (1/2) m v^2
m = mass, v = velocity.
Derivation of E_k = (1/2) m v^2 (using suvat and F = ma):
Object of mass m accelerates uniformly from rest (u = 0) to velocity v.
From v^2 = u^2 + 2as → v^2 = 2as → a = v^2 / (2s).
F = ma = m × v^2 / (2s) = (m v^2) / (2s).
Work done = F × s = (m v^2) / (2s) × s = (1/2) m v^2 = kinetic energy.
Gravitational Potential Energy (ΔE_p) – depends on object's position in a gravitational field and its mass.
Elastic Potential Energy – depends on elasticity/spring constant and extension/compression. Both are stored forms of energy but arise from different mechanisms.
Change in gravitational potential energy close to Earth's surface (uniform field):
ΔE_p = m g Δh
m = mass, g = acceleration due to gravity (~9.81 m s^-2), Δh = change in height.
Derivation of ΔE_p = m g Δh:
To lift an object at constant velocity, applied force = weight = m g.
Work done = Force × distance = m g × Δh.
This work done equals the increase in gravitational potential energy.
Note: the force does not have to be vertically upwards – just ensure the change in vertical height is used (may involve trigonometry).

Example (pendulum):
Pendulum mass 500 g (0.5 kg), rises 10 cm (0.1 m) at maximum amplitude. Ignore air resistance.
At maximum amplitude, all energy is GPE: ΔE_p = m g Δh = 0.5 × 9.81 × 0.1 = 0.4905 J.
At equilibrium position, all GPE is converted to KE: (1/2) m v^2 = 0.4905 J.
(1/2) × 0.5 × v^2 = 0.4905 → v^2 = 1.962 → v = 1.4 m s^-1.

Topic 6: Deformation of Solids
6.1 - Stress and Strain
Deformation – a change in shape of an object due to an applied force.
Types of forces and deformation:
Tensile – causes an object to be stretched (tensile deformation).
Compressive – causes an object to be squashed / compressed (compressive deformation).
Hooke's law – extension is directly proportional to the force applied, provided environmental conditions (e.g. temperature) are kept constant.
Force-extension graph is a straight line through the origin.
Limit of proportionality (P) – the point after which Hooke's law is no longer obeyed.
Elastic limit (E) – just after the limit of proportionality; beyond this point, the material will deform plastically (permanently stretched).
Hooke's law equation: F = k x
F = force / load applied
k = spring constant (measure of stiffness)
x = extension
Rearranged: k = F / x

Tensile stress = force applied per unit cross-sectional area.
Stress = F / A
Tensile strain = extension over original length.
Strain = x / L (where x = extension, L = original length)
Young modulus (E) – a value describing the stiffness of a material.
Up to the limit of proportionality (for a material obeying Hooke's law), stress is proportional to strain, so stress / strain is constant. This constant is the Young modulus.
Young modulus = Tensile stress / Tensile strain
Using formulas: E = (F / A) / (x / L) = F L / (x A)
On a stress-strain graph, the Young modulus is the gradient of the straight (linear) portion of the graph.

Experiment to measure the Young modulus of a metal wire:
Setup:
Set up apparatus as shown in the diagram (wire clamped, with masses attached at one end, and a marker on the wire alongside a metre ruler).
Procedure:
Measure the diameter of the wire in 3 different places using a micrometer. Record these values.
Attach the metre ruler to the workbench so the lower end faces the G-clamp. Place a marker on the wire at 0 cm on the ruler.
Measure the length of wire from the blocks of wood to the marker on the wire when it is taut (this is the original length L).
Attach a mass to the wire and record the total mass (in kg). The wire stretches, so the marker moves – record the new position of the marker.
Add another 100 g mass and again record the position of the marker. Repeat until you have readings for at least 7 mass values.
Calculations:
Find the mean diameter and calculate the average cross-sectional area:
A = π d^2 / 4
For each mass, calculate the force: F = m g (record in a table).
Calculate the extension for each mass: final marker position – initial position.
Calculate stress for each mass: stress = F / A.
Calculate strain for each mass: strain = x / L.
Plot a graph of stress against strain and draw a line of best fit.
Since Young modulus = stress / strain, the gradient of the line of best fit equals the Young modulus of the metal.

6.2 - Elastic and Plastic Behaviour
Elastic deformation – material returns to its original shape when the applied force is removed. All work done is stored as elastic strain energy.
Plastic deformation – material's shape is changed permanently. Work is done to move atoms apart, so energy is not only stored as elastic strain energy but also dissipated as heat.

Elastic strain energy:
When work is done to stretch or compress a material, energy is stored as elastic strain energy.
This cannot be calculated using W = F s cosθ because the force is variable.
Instead, it is the area under a force-extension graph.
For a straight-line graph (within the limit of proportionality):
Elastic strain energy = (1/2) F x = (1/2) k x^2
This equation applies only when deforming within the limit of proportionality.

Force-extension graph behaviours:
Plastic – material experiences large extension as load increases, especially beyond the elastic limit.
Brittle – material extends very little and is likely to fracture (break apart) at a low extension.
Loading and unloading:
When stretched beyond the elastic limit, the unloading line does not return to the origin.
The loading and unloading lines are parallel because the material's stiffness (spring constant) remains constant.
The area between the loading and unloading lines represents the work done to permanently deform the material.

Topic 7: Waves
7.1 - Progressive Waves
Key terms:



Wave equation:
v = f λ (speed = frequency × wavelength)
Derivation:
A wave travels a distance equal to its wavelength (λ) in one time period (T), so v = λ / T.
Since f = 1 / T, substitute to get v = f λ.

Using a CRO (cathode-ray oscilloscope) to measure frequency and amplitude:
To find frequency:
CRO displays the sound wave's displacement-time graph. Adjust the time-base settings so the waveforms fill as much of the screen as possible.
Measure the number of full waves on the screen and the number of divisions they occupy. Multiply the number of divisions by the time-base to find the total time, then calculate period T = time taken for one full oscillation.
Use f = 1 / T to find frequency.
To find amplitude:
The y-axis shows displacement. Adjust the voltage/division setting so the wave fills the vertical space.
Measure the number of divisions the wave spans vertically and multiply by the voltage/division to obtain the amplitude (in volts).

Intensity:
Intensity (I) = power per unit area → I = P / A
P = power, A = area.
Intensity of a wave is directly proportional to amplitude squared (I ∝ A²).

7.2 - Transverse and Longitudinal Waves
Transverse waves – oscillations of particles (or fields) are at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction of energy transfer.
All electromagnetic (EM) waves are transverse.
EM waves travel at 3 × 10^8 m s^-1 in a vacuum.
Examples: shaking a slinky vertically, waves on a string attached to a signal generator.
Longitudinal waves – oscillations of particles are parallel to the direction of energy transfer.
Made up of compressions (regions of high pressure/density) and rarefactions (regions of low pressure/density).
Cannot travel in a vacuum.
Example: sound waves. Can be demonstrated by pushing a slinky horizontally.

Two types of wave graphs:
Displacement-distance graph – shows how displacement of a particle varies with distance along the wave. Used to measure wavelength.
For a transverse wave, this graph looks similar to the actual wave.
For a longitudinal wave, the graph looks very different from the actual wave.
Displacement-time graph – shows how displacement of a particle varies with time. Used to measure the period of a wave.

7.3 - Doppler Effect for Sound Waves
Doppler effect – the compression or spreading out of waves emitted or reflected by a moving source.
Wavelengths in front of the source are compressed (shorter λ, higher observed frequency).
Wavelengths behind the source are spread out (longer λ, lower observed frequency).
Example: the sound of a car moving past you.
Observed frequency equation (source moving relative to stationary observer):
f_o = (f_s × v) / (v ± v_s)
Where:
f_o = observed frequency
f_s = frequency emitted by the source
v = speed of sound
v_s = speed of the source (constant)
Sign convention:
v_s is added when the source is moving away from the observer (denominator larger → lower f_o).
v_s is subtracted when the source is moving towards the observer (denominator smaller → higher f_o).
Memory aid: towards = minus (higher pitch), away = plus (lower pitch).

7.4 - Electromagnetic Spectrum
The EM spectrum contains all electromagnetic waves, classified into principal categories by wavelength.
Orders of magnitude (approximate wavelengths):



Visible light: wavelengths in the range 400 – 700 nm are visible to the human eye.
All EM waves travel at the same speed in free space: 3 × 10^8 m s^-1 (speed of light, c).

7.5 - Polarisation
Polarised wave – oscillates in only one plane (e.g. only vertically, if vertically polarised).
Only transverse waves can be polarised – polarisation provides evidence for the transverse nature of waves, because polarisation can only occur if oscillations are perpendicular to the direction of travel.
Effect on intensity:
Passing through a polarising filter excludes waves of certain orientations.
Intensity after filtering is always less than or equal to the intensity before.
Malus' Law:
I = I₀ cos²θ
I₀ = initial intensity (before filter)
I = intensity after passing through the filter
θ = angle between the polarisation direction of the wave and the axis of the filter

Topic 8: Superposition
8.1 - Stationary Waves
Superposition – occurs when two (or more) waves of the same type cross each other. The resultant displacement is the vector sum of the individual displacements.
Types of interference:
Constructive interference – waves meet in phase (phase difference = 0, 2π, 4π, ...). Their displacements add together to produce a larger resultant displacement.
Destructive interference – waves meet out of phase / antiphase (phase difference = π, 3π, 5π, ...). Their displacements cancel each other out to produce a smaller (or zero) resultant displacement.
Stationary wave (standing wave) – formed from the superposition of two progressive waves travelling in opposite directions in the same plane, with the same frequency, wavelength and amplitude.
No energy is transferred by a stationary wave.
Nodes – regions of minimum displacement (destructive interference, waves meet out of phase).
Antinodes – regions of maximum displacement (constructive interference, waves meet in phase).
The distance between adjacent nodes (or adjacent antinodes) = half a wavelength (λ/2) for any harmonic.
Fundamental frequency (first harmonic) – the lowest frequency at which a stationary wave forms. It has two nodes and one antinode.
For a vibrating string: f = (1 / 2L) × √(T / μ)
L = length of the vibrating string
T = tension in the string
μ = mass per unit length (kg m^-1)
Harmonics:
First harmonic (fundamental) – 1 antinode → frequency = f
Second harmonic – 2 antinodes → frequency = 2f
Third harmonic – 3 antinodes → frequency = 3f
... and so on for the nth harmonic (frequency = n × f).
Examples of stationary waves:
Microwaves – formed by reflecting a microwave beam at a metal plate. Use a microwave probe to detect nodes and antinodes.
Sound waves – formed by placing a speaker at one end of a closed glass tube. Lay powder across the bottom – it is shaken at antinodes and settles at nodes. Distance between nodes = λ/2. With known frequency f, use v = f λ to find the speed of sound in air.

8.2 - Diffraction
Diffraction – the spreading out of waves when they pass through or around a gap.
Greatest diffraction occurs when the gap size ≈ wavelength.
When gap < wavelength → most waves are reflected.
When gap > wavelength → less noticeable diffraction.
When a wave meets an obstacle → diffraction round the edges; the wider the obstacle compared to the wavelength, the less diffraction.
Single-slit diffraction (monochromatic light):
Forms an interference pattern of light and dark fringes on a screen.
Central bright fringe – twice the width of all other fringes.
Bright fringes – constructive interference (waves meet in phase).
Dark fringes – destructive interference (waves meet completely out of phase).
Intensity of fringes decreases as you move away from the central fringe.

8.3 - Interference
Coherent light source – has the same frequency and wavelength, and a fixed phase difference.
Lasers are coherent and monochromatic (emit a single wavelength or small range), so they form clear interference patterns.
Young's double-slit experiment – demonstrates interference of light from two sources.
Use two coherent sources, or one coherent source with a double slit.
If using a non-coherent source (e.g. light bulb), place a single slit before the double slit to create a fixed path difference, and a filter to make the light monochromatic.
Procedure:
Shine coherent light through two slits, where the slit width is about the same size as the wavelength (so light diffracts).
Each slit acts as a coherent point source, producing a pattern of light and dark fringes.
Bright fringes (constructive) occur where path difference = nλ (n = 0, 1, 2, ...).
Dark fringes (destructive) occur where path difference = (n + 1/2)λ.
Formula: λ = a x / D
λ = wavelength of light
a = slit separation (distance between the two slits)
x = fringe spacing (distance between adjacent bright fringes)
D = distance from slits to screen

8.4 - The Diffraction Grating
Diffraction grating – a slide containing many equally spaced slits very close together.
The interference pattern is sharper and brighter than with a double slit (more rays reinforcing the pattern) → measurements are more accurate.
As the number of slits increases, the maxima become narrower and brighter.
Orders of maxima:
Zero order – central bright fringe (n = 0).
First order – maxima either side of zero order (n = ±1).
Second order – outside first order (n = ±2), and so on.
Diffraction grating formula: d sinθ = nλ
d = distance between adjacent slits (slit spacing)
θ = angle from the normal to the maximum
n = order of the maximum (integer)
λ = wavelength
Derivation (for first order maximum):
For the first order maximum (n = 1), the path difference between adjacent rays = λ.
A right-angled triangle is formed with hypotenuse d and opposite side λ.
Using sinθ = opposite / hypotenuse → sinθ = λ / d → d sinθ = λ.
For higher orders, path difference = nλ → d sinθ = nλ.
Key points:
As λ increases (e.g. changing from blue to red light), the distance between orders increases (θ increases → pattern spreads out).
The maximum value of sinθ is 1, so any value of n where sinθ > 1 is impossible (i.e. higher orders may not exist).
Applications of diffraction gratings:
Astronomy – splitting light from stars to obtain line absorption spectra, revealing which elements are present.
X-ray crystallography – x-rays directed at a thin crystal sheet. The crystal acts as a diffraction grating (since x-ray wavelengths are similar in size to atomic spacing). The diffraction pattern can be used to measure atomic spacing in materials.

Topic 9: Electricity
9.1 - Electric Current
Electric current (I) – the flow of charge (Q) per unit time (t), i.e. the rate of flow of charge.
I = Q / t
Charge is carried by charge carriers in quantised amounts. Typically the charge carriers are electrons, each carrying the elementary charge e = 1.60 × 10^-19 C (coulombs).
Total charge transferred: Q = I × t
Example: a conductor delivering 5 A for 10 s → Q = 5 × 10 = 50 C.
Derivation of I = A n v q:
Consider a current-carrying conductor with:
A = cross-sectional area
n = number density of charge carriers (number per unit volume)
v = mean drift velocity of charge carriers
q = charge on each carrier
In time Δt, the distance travelled by charge carriers = v × Δt.Volume of charge carriers passing a point = A × v × Δt.Number of charge carriers = n × A × v × Δt.Total charge passing = n × A × v × Δt × q.Current = total charge / time = (n A v Δt q) / Δt = I = A n v q

9.2 - Potential Difference and Power
Potential difference (V) – the energy transferred per unit charge between two points in a circuit.
V = W / Q
W = energy transferred / work done (J)
Q = charge (C)
Power (P) – the energy transferred per unit time (rate of energy transfer).
P = E / t (E = energy transferred, t = time)
Also: P = V × I
Combining with V = I R (Ohm's law), we get:
P = V I = V^2 / R = I^2 R
Energy transferred: E = P × t = V × I × t

9.3 - Resistance and Resistivity
Resistance (R) – a measure of how difficult it is for charge carriers to pass through a component.
R = V / I
Ohm's law – for an ohmic conductor, current is directly proportional to potential difference across it, provided physical conditions (e.g. temperature) are kept constant.
Ohmic conductor – follows Ohm's law; its I-V graph is a straight line through the origin (at constant temperature).
Semiconductor diode:
Forward bias – allows current to flow easily once the threshold voltage is exceeded.
Reverse bias – resistance is extremely high, so only a very small (negligible) current can flow.
Filament lamp:
Contains a metal wire that heats up as current increases → resistance increases with current.
At low currents, the wire does not heat significantly → Ohm's law is obeyed (linear region).
As current increases, the graph curves due to increasing resistance (gradient decreases).
Ammeters – assumed to have zero resistance (do not affect circuit current measurements).
Voltmeters – assumed to have infinite resistance (no current flows through them, so voltage measurements are exact).

Resistivity (ρ) – a measure of how strongly a material opposes the flow of electric current. It is independent of the size of the sample (depends only on the material and temperature).
ρ = R A / L
R = resistance
A = cross-sectional area
L = length
Resistivity is the resistance of a material of length 1 m and cross-sectional area 1 m².

Effect of temperature on resistance:
Metal conductor – as temperature increases, resistance increases.
Reason: atoms gain kinetic energy and vibrate more → charge carriers (electrons) collide with atoms more frequently → slow down → current decreases → resistance increases (R = V / I).
Thermistor (NTC – negative temperature coefficient) – as temperature increases, resistance decreases.
Reason: increasing temperature causes electrons to be emitted from atoms → number of charge carriers increases → current increases → resistance decreases.
Application: temperature sensor – can trigger an event (e.g. turn on heating) when temperature drops below a set value.

Superconductor – a material with zero resistivity below a certain temperature, called the critical temperature (T_c). Most known superconductors have critical temperatures close to 0 K (-273°C).
With zero resistivity, resistance also drops to zero.
Applications:
Power cables – zero energy loss due to heating during transmission.
Strong magnetic fields – no constant power source needed (e.g. maglev trains – no friction between trains and rails).
Medical applications (e.g. MRI scanners).

Light Dependent Resistor (LDR) – resistance decreases with increasing light intensity.
Application: can be used as a light sensor to trigger events.
Example circuit: LDR in series with a fixed resistor R₂. If light intensity falls, LDR resistance increases → total resistance increases → circuit current decreases → voltage across R₂ decreases (V = IR) → p.d. out decreases.
To reverse the effect, swap the positions of the LDR and the fixed resistor → p.d. out increases as light intensity decreases.
This can be used to switch on a light bulb when a certain threshold voltage is reached.

Topic 10: D.C. Circuits
10.1 - Practical Circuits
Internal resistance (r) – caused by electrons colliding with atoms inside a battery, resulting in some energy being lost before the electrons leave the battery. Represented as a small resistor inside the battery.
Electromotive force (emf / ε) – the energy transferred by a cell per coulomb of charge that passes through it.
ε = E / Q (E = energy transferred, Q = charge)
Total resistance in a circuit with internal resistance:
R_T = R + r
R = load resistance (external)
r = internal resistance
emf = total resistance × current:
ε = I (R + r) = I R + I r
Terminal p.d. (V) – the p.d. across the load resistance R:
V = I R
Lost volts (v) – the p.d. across the internal resistance r (energy wasted by the cell per coulomb of charge):
v = I r
Therefore: ε = V + v (emf = terminal p.d. + lost volts)
The emf of a battery can be measured by connecting a voltmeter across the cell when no current flows (open circuit) – the reading equals the emf.

10.2 - Kirchhoff's Laws
Kirchhoff's first law – the total current flowing into a junction equals the total current flowing out of that junction.
This shows that charge is conserved (no charge is lost at any point).
Kirchhoff's second law – the sum of all the voltages (p.d.s) in a series circuit equals the supply voltage (or the sum of all voltages around any closed loop is zero).
This shows that energy is conserved (no energy is lost at any point).

Series circuits:
Current is the same everywhere.
Battery p.d. is shared across all components.
Total voltage: V_T = V_1 + V_2 + V_3 + ...
Total resistance: R_T = R_1 + R_2 + R_3 + ...
Parallel circuits:
The sum of currents in each parallel branch equals the total current.
The p.d. across each branch is the same.
For identical cells in parallel, total voltage equals the voltage of one cell (current is split equally between branches).
V_T = V_1 = V_2 = V_3 = ...
Total resistance (for resistors in parallel):
1 / R_T = 1 / R_1 + 1 / R_2 + 1 / R_3 + ...
Example (combined series and parallel):
Find total resistance of a circuit with a 4Ω and 16Ω resistor in parallel, in series with a 10Ω and 3Ω resistor.
Parallel combination: 1 / R_T = 1/4 + 1/16 = 5/16 → R_T = 3.2 Ω
Total: R_T = 10 + 3 + 3.2 = 16.2 Ω

10.3 - Potential Dividers
Potential divider – a circuit with several resistors in series connected across a voltage source, used to produce a required fraction of the source p.d. that remains constant.
Variable potential divider – use a variable resistor as one of the resistors in series. By varying its resistance, you can vary the output p.d.
If resistance across R_1 increases:
Total circuit resistance increases → circuit current decreases.
Using V = I R, the voltage across R_2 decreases → output p.d. decreases.

Sensors as potential dividers:
Replace the variable resistor with a thermistor (temperature sensor) or LDR (light sensor).
LDR – resistance decreases as light intensity increases.
Example (LDR as a light sensor):
If light intensity falls → LDR resistance increases → total circuit resistance increases → circuit current decreases.
Using V = I R, voltage across R_2 decreases → p.d. out decreases.
To reverse the effect, swap the LDR and the fixed resistor → p.d. out increases as light intensity decreases.
This circuit can be used to switch on a light bulb when it gets dark (once a threshold voltage is met).

Potentiometer:
Consists of a voltmeter and a three-terminal resistor.
Has a control to adjust the resistance between terminals, while resistance between the two outer terminals remains fixed.
Used to compare potential differences in parts of a circuit, or as a variable resistor in a potential divider circuit.

Null measurements:
Used to measure circuit features more accurately (since ammeters and voltmeters influence current flow and alter circuit behaviour).
A galvanometer can measure emf directly using: ε = V + I r
With current set to zero (I = 0), emf = cell voltage (direct measurement).
By knowing emf and measuring voltage, a galvanometer can be placed along a length of wire to measure current and determine resistance.

Topic 11: Particle Physics
11.1 - Atoms, Nuclei, and Radiation
Rutherford scattering – demonstrated the existence of the nucleus. Before this, scientists believed in Thomson's plum pudding model, which stated that the atom was a sphere of positive charge with small areas of negative charge evenly distributed throughout.
Rutherford's apparatus:
Alpha source and gold foil in an evacuated chamber.
Chamber covered with a fluorescent coating to show where alpha particles hit.
A movable microscope to observe the paths of the alpha particles.
Expected results (if plum pudding model were true):
Positively charged alpha particles would be deflected by very small amounts when passing through the foil.
Actual observations:
Most alpha particles passed straight through with no deflection → atom is mostly empty space (not uniform density).
A small amount were deflected by large angles → centre of the atom is positively charged (repelled the positively charged alpha particles).
Very few were deflected back by more than 90° → centre of the atom is very dense (could deflect fast-moving alpha particles) and very small (very few particles were deflected by large angles).
Conclusion: the atom has a small, dense, positively charged nucleus at its centre.

Atomic structure:
An atom consists of protons, neutrons (in the nucleus, collectively called nucleons), and electrons (orbiting the nucleus in shells).
Properties of subatomic particles:



Specific charge = charge / mass (charge-to-mass ratio).
Example (proton): specific charge = (1.6 × 10^-19) / (1.67 × 10^-27) = 9.58 × 10^7 C kg^-1

Nuclear notation:
Proton number (Z) = number of protons.
Nucleon number (A) = number of protons + neutrons.
Shown as: A/Z X (where X is the element symbol).
Conservation laws in nuclear processes:
Total charge is conserved.
Total number of nucleons is conserved.
Total lepton number (electrons + neutrinos) is conserved.
Isotopes – atoms with the same number of protons (same Z) but different numbers of neutrons (different A).
Example: carbon-14 is a radioactive isotope used in carbon dating (calculating the percentage of remaining carbon-14 and using its known half-life to estimate age).

Radiation – where an unstable nucleus emits energy (EM waves or subatomic particles) to become more stable.
Unified atomic mass unit (u) – equivalent to the mass of one nucleon (proton or neutron): 1 u = 1.6 × 10^-27 kg.
Elementary charge (e) – magnitude of charge on one electron or one proton: e = 1.6 × 10^-19 C.
Antiparticle – has the same mass but opposite charge and quantum numbers to its corresponding particle. Example: the positron is the antiparticle of the electron.

Types of radiation:




Alpha decay:
An alpha particle (2 protons + 2 neutrons) is emitted from a nucleus with too many nucleons.
Nucleon number decreases by 4, proton number decreases by 2.
Example: 238/92 U → 234/90 Th + 4/2 α
Alpha particles have discrete energy (equal to the energy difference between the original nucleus and the product).
Beta-minus decay (β⁻):
Occurs when a nucleus has too many neutrons compared to protons.
A neutron decays into a proton + electron + antineutrino.
Proton number increases by 1, nucleon number stays the same.
Example: 228/88 Ra → 228/89 Ac + 0/-1 β (plus antineutrino)
Beta-plus decay (β⁺):
Occurs when a nucleus has too many protons compared to neutrons.
A proton decays into a neutron + positron + neutrino.
Proton number decreases by 1, nucleon number stays the same.
Example: 11/6 C → 11/5 B + 0/+1 β (plus neutrino)
Beta particles have a continuous range of energies because the energy from decay is shared between the beta particle and the neutrino/antineutrino.

11.2 - Fundamental Particles
Quarks – fundamental particles that combine to form hadrons (e.g. protons and neutrons).
Six flavours (types): up, down, top, bottom, charm, strange.


Each quark has an antiquark with the opposite charge (denoted with a bar, e.g. ū for anti-up).

Hadrons – particles made of quarks. There are two types:
Baryons – composed of three quarks.
Proton = uud (charge: +2/3 + 2/3 - 1/3 = +1e)
Neutron = udd (charge: +2/3 - 1/3 - 1/3 = 0e)
Mesons – composed of one quark and one antiquark.
Example: π⁺ meson = u d̄ (up quark + anti-down quark)

Quark changes in beta decay:
Beta-minus (β⁻) – a neutron decays into a proton:
A down quark changes into an up quark.
n (udd) → p (uud) + e⁻ + antineutrino
Beta-plus (β⁺) – a proton decays into a neutron:
An up quark changes into a down quark.
p (uud) → n (udd) + e⁺ + neutrino

Summary of particle classifications:
Hadrons – made of quarks (experience strong nuclear force). Includes:
Baryons (3 quarks) – e.g. protons, neutrons
Mesons (quark + antiquark) – e.g. pions
Leptons – fundamental particles NOT made of quarks (e.g. electrons, neutrinos). Do NOT experience the strong nuclear force.
Quarks – fundamental particles that make up hadrons.
